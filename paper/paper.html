The justice-held population, England and Wales

A quarterly composite series of people whose liberty is conditioned by the criminal justice system

Hamish Paton  ·  Walkforward Research

Version 1.0, 5 September 2026  ·  Tag: Measured  ·  Licence CC BY 4.0  ·  DOI 10.5281/zenodo.22536356

Suggested citation Walk Forward Research (2026). The justice-held population, England and Wales, 2019 to 2026: methodology and series, v1.0. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22536356

1Definition

The justice-held population is the number of people in England and Wales who, at a point in time, are in one of four states:

Table 1. The four limbs.
LimbStateSource
AIn prison (all custody types, including remand)MoJ Offender Management Statistics, prison population tables
BUnder Probation Service supervision in the community, on a court order or on post-release licenceMoJ OMSQ, probation caseload, “All supervision in the community”
COn bail awaiting trial in the Crown CourtMoJ Criminal Court Statistics Quarterly, Table O2, remanded on bail
DAwaiting trial or sending in the magistrates’ courts on an either-way or indictable-only chargeMoJ Criminal Court Statistics Quarterly, Table M1, “for trial” open cases

Justice-held population = A + B + C + D. Published at calendar year end from 2019 and at the latest available quarter. Designed to be reproducible from published tables by anyone.

Youth justice, immigration detention, civil ordersOutside scope

Between December 2019 and March 2026 the series rose by 74,990 (23.3%). Of that increase, 6% was prison, 5% community supervision, 41% Crown Court bail and 48% magistrates’ cases. People awaiting trial with a realistic custody outcome (C + D) numbered 133,295 in March 2026, 1.5 times the prison population.

4Relationship to existing measures

Prison plus probation (A + B) is not new. The Council of Europe SPACE I and II statistics compute a “correctional rate” of prison plus probation per 100,000 population for every member state. McNeill and Beyens (2013) and McNeill (2018) built the mass supervision literature on that comparison. The US Bureau of Justice Statistics has published prison + jail + probation + parole annually since 1985.

On this construction, A + B per 100,000 population for England and Wales was 416 in 2024. McNeill (2021) cites Scotland at 548 and describes that as 125% of England and Wales, implying about 440. The two are consistent within definitional differences, so limbs A and B here are comparable with the international literature.

Limbs C and D are the addition. No existing series counts people released on bail awaiting trial. The US measure includes pre-trial detainees (in jail) but not defendants released pending trial. SPACE II covers probation only. Limbs C and D account for 89% of the growth in the England and Wales series since 2019.

5Adjustments and breaks

Probation, 31 March 2026. The MoJ introduced a new data pipeline from the January to March 2026 reporting period, which captures more cases and was not applied retrospectively (OMSQ probation tables, note 49). The break is estimated as the last quarterly change less the mean of the three preceding quarterly changes: +3,623 on “All supervision in the community” (2.1% of level). The March 2026 figure in Table 3 is the published 179,300 less 3,623. The underlying quarterly growth before the break was about +2,180, so the community limb was genuinely rising through 2025.

Magistrates’ series start. Table M1 with the offence-class split begins in 2019 Q1, which fixes the start of the four-limb series. A three-limb series (A + B + C) is available from 2015 and shows the same direction.

Crown Court bail, 2015. Table O2 begins in 2016 Q1; the 2015 three-limb figure uses that value.

6Known limitations

  • Cases, not people, in limbs C and D. A defendant with two open cases counts twice. MoJ has stated that the same is true of recall counts. The overstatement is unquantified.
  • Overlap between limbs. A person on licence (B) who is charged with a new offence and bailed (C or D) is counted twice. Unquantified; would reduce the total.
  • Custody in limb D. A small number of defendants awaiting magistrates’ trial are held in custody and are therefore also in limb A. Custody time limits at magistrates’ courts are 56 days, so the overlap is small.
  • Probation “contact suspended” cases. In March 2026, 38,896 of limb B were cases where contact supervision had been suspended under Probation Reset or Probation Impact. They remain on an active order or licence and are included, but they are not being actively supervised.
  • Data quality on remand. HMCTS has told the Public Accounts Committee that MoJ data on the remand population are not accurate enough to draw insights from. Limb A totals reconcile across weekly and monthly publications; time-on-remand data do not enter this series.

7Planned refinements

  • A custody-weighted version of limb D using the plea and offence tables, so that summary non-motoring cases with a realistic custody outcome can be included.
  • De-duplication of limbs C and D to persons, if MoJ publishes or releases defendant-level counts.
  • Extension of limb D back before 2019 if an earlier consistent table can be located.
  • Quarterly publication on the day the OMSQ and CCSQ tables are released.

8Why the series exists

The prison population of England and Wales has been within a few per cent of 85,000 for fifteen years. Over the same period sentences lengthened, release points were cut three times in two years, the remand population doubled and then fell as courts bailed defendants they would previously have remanded, and recalls to prison rose until they exceeded releases. Each of these is reported separately as a record. None of them is visible in the headline, because the headline is managed to the number of cells available.

The point of the series

The justice-held population is an attempt to measure what the headline does not: how many people the state is currently holding in some condition of restricted liberty, whether in a cell, on a licence, on an order, or waiting for a courtroom. It has grown by a quarter since 2019, and almost all of the growth is in the queue.

What would prove this wrong

  • Prior work counting pre-trial defendants alongside supervised populations. None was found in the mass supervision literature, SPACE or BJS; the author of Pervasive Punishment has been asked directly.
  • A person-level count of the court limbs materially lower than the case count. This would reduce the level, not the direction: the case count rose 161% for Crown Court bail.
  • A definitional change in the probation caseload larger than the 3,623 estimated here. The MoJ has not published a back-series; the estimate is from the quarterly path and is stated.

9Reproducibility

All four limbs are published MoJ tables. The construction is implemented in the Walk Forward Research repository (justice_held_population.csv, probation_break_adjustment.csv, magistrates_open_by_class.csv, crown_court_open_by_remand.csv) at github.com/walkforwardresearch/prisoncap and refreshes from the gov.uk content API. The series and components are deposited at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.22536356.
